Did the ball move, or did the camera?

Add camera shake and measurement error to the same soccer-ball motion. Can three methods recover its path? Predict first, then run a repeatable experiment to find out whether smoother also means more accurate.

Synthetic data · Runs on this page · No video upload · No API key

Turn a game problem into a research question

Imagine building a soccer replay game. The camera shakes, and the ball seems to change direction. Your challenge: recover the ball’s actual position and explain why your method works.

Observed position=True ball position+Camera translation+Measurement noise

Four fixed landmarks stay in place on the field. Compare their observed positions with their reference positions to estimate how far the camera shifted in this frame.

Keep today’s question small enough to test

We start with synthetic ball coordinates. We are not detecting or tracking a ball in real video. This model only simulates 2D translation: no rotation, zoom, occlusion, or perspective. Smoothing a trajectory is not stabilizing video frames. This page does not train an AI model.

The simulator provides the true position and actual camera shift as answer keys. Your method can use observations and landmarks, but cannot peek at those answers. Later research can explore video annotation, object detection, and matching objects across frames.

Which CSV columns are inputs, and which are answer keys?

Inputs: frame, time_s, observed_x/y, plus four sets of landmark_1…4_reference_x/y and landmark_1…4_observed_x/y.

Answer keys: truth_x/y, camera_dx/dy. Use these only for evaluation and checking the simulator. Real video does not automatically supply ground truth.

Method outputs: estimated_camera_dx/dy, compensated_x/y, smoothed_x/y, smoothing_samples. The Python example recomputes these values; it does not read these output columns as predictions.

3 Make AI your experiment partner

Give your CSV and the prompt below to an AI assistant you already use. This page does not call AI automatically. You choose the question, check the code, run comparisons, and decide what the evidence supports.

  1. Your job: Predict first, choose one factor, and explain why the comparison is fair.
  2. AI helps: Explain code, suggest one change, and list ways it might fail.
  3. Check together: Check for leaked answer keys or future frames. You run the code and compare it with the baseline.
  4. You decide: Explain one improvement and one counterexample. Save your AI conversation and experiment records.

Three next-step challenges
  1. Zero-noise challenge: Set noise to 0. What error should camera compensation achieve? Is a larger window always better?
  2. Fair-comparison challenge: Keep the seed and shake fixed. Compare windows 1, 3, 7, and 15. Plot RMSE against window size.
  3. Counterexample challenge: Set camera shake to 0. Could landmark measurement errors make compensation worse than doing nothing?

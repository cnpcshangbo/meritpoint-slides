The Story

The machine the press said would walk and talk

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt unveiled the Perceptron, and the New York Times reported it was the embryo of a computer expected to "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence." It did none of those things. Underneath the headlines the Perceptron was a simple classifier that draws a straight line between two groups of points — very nearly what you'll build today. The hype collapsed, funding vanished, and the field entered its first winter. The humble idea survived it, and is now inside almost everything.
Neural voice · click to play / pause

Two lessons in one story: AI hype is as old as AI itself — and the boring idea underneath the hype is often the one that lasts.

Worth being precise: the "walk, talk, see, write" line is the NYT paraphrasing the Navy's own press release, not Rosenblatt's paper. His actual claims were narrower. This matters — the pattern isn't "researcher lies," it's "modest result + institutional press office + eager reporter = a myth that outlives the machine." Ask the room where they saw that pattern last month.
Concept 1 · the shape of the problem

Predicting a category

📈 Regression (Class 10)

The target is a number on a scale — 27.4 metres, $412,000, 68 points. "How much?"

🎯 Classification (today)

The target is a label from a set — real object / false alarm, spam / not spam, benign / malignant. "Which one?"

KEY IDEA — logistic regression doesn't output a label directly. It outputs a probability between 0 and 1, and you choose the cut-off that turns it into a decision. That extra step looks like a technicality. It is the most consequential design choice in the whole model.
"Binary" = two classes; multi-class is a backup slide, not core today. The probability-then-threshold split is worth writing down — it is what makes classifiers tunable, it's why the same trained model can be cautious or aggressive, and it's what the second half of the hour and the whole discussion segment hang on.
Concept 2 · the one new piece of machinery

The sigmoid: a score becomes a probability

Keep Class 10's line. Feed its output — call it z — through one S-shaped squashing function, and whatever comes out is between 0 and 1.

z = w·x + b
P(class = 1) = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

z = 0 → exactly 0.50, maximum uncertainty. Large positive z → confidently 1. Large negative z → confidently 0. It never actually reaches 0 or 1, which is the model's way of never being certain.

One more thing changes with it: squared error stops being the right loss. Classification uses cross-entropy (log loss) — it punishes confident wrong answers brutally, and unlike MSE-through-a-sigmoid it gives gradient descent a bowl-shaped landscape with a single bottom instead of a lumpy one full of false valleys.
The curve is plotted from the real function at 0.2 intervals; the labelled dots are σ(−4)=0.02, σ(−2)=0.12, σ(0)=0.50, σ(2)=0.88, σ(4)=0.98. Two claims to make carefully: cross-entropy's convexity is the technical reason (MSE composed with sigmoid is non-convex), and "punishes confident wrong answers" is the intuitive one — a prediction of 0.99 on a true 0 costs about 4.6, a prediction of 0.6 costs about 0.9.
Check · reading a probability

Quick check · tap an answer

Your classifier returns P(real object) = 0.52 for a detection. What has the model told you?

0.52 is the model's honest way of saying "I have no idea." It clears the default 0.5 threshold and gets labelled REAL — which is why reporting only the final label throws away the most useful thing the model produced. In a car, a 0.52 and a 0.99 should not trigger the same action.

Option D is deliberately seductive and NOT crazy — a well-calibrated model does have that frequency interpretation. It's marked wrong here because untuned logistic-regression probabilities are usually poorly calibrated; if a sharp student argues for D, that's a win, and 'calibration' is the word for what they're describing.
Concept 3 · the idea in one picture

The decision boundary

Set σ(z) = 0.5 and you're setting z = 0 — and z = 0 is just a straight line through your feature space. Everything on one side is predicted real; everything on the other, false alarm.

  • The boundary IS the model. Two weights and an intercept, drawn.
  • Points near the line are the 0.5-ish, uncertain ones
  • Move your threshold off 0.5 and the line slides — same model, different caution

● real objects · ■ false alarms · the line is fitted at build time by gradient descent on all 50 rows of the detection log — it agrees with scikit-learn's LogisticRegression to three decimals: 2.24·confidence + 2.17·(height/100) − 2.25, with 47/50 points on the correct side.

Point at the three or four dots sitting on the wrong side of the line — that's not a bug, it's what real data looks like, and a model that got all 50 right on data this noisy would be memorising. Then point at a dot NEAR the line versus one far from it: same predicted label, wildly different confidence. That contrast is the setup for the threshold discussion.
Concept 4 · the number that lies to you

96% accurate and completely useless

200 dashcam frames. 8 contain a pedestrian. Ship a model that answers "no pedestrian" every single time and never looks at the image:

accuracy = 192 / 200 = 96.0%
pedestrians found = 0 / 8

Nothing is broken. The metric is simply answering a question nobody asked.

Report these instead

Recall — of the real pedestrians, what fraction did we catch? (here: 0.00)

Precision — of the alarms we raised, what fraction were real?

Confusion matrix — all four boxes: hits, misses, false alarms, correct rejections.

Whenever one class is rare, accuracy flatters. Rare is the normal case in anything that matters — disease, fraud, equipment failure, pedestrians. Class 14 gives these metrics a full hour; today the rule is simply: never report accuracy alone on imbalanced data.
Lab 2 prints this exact 96% result so the number isn't just asserted. The precision/recall trade-off is genuinely one-directional: you can always buy recall with precision by lowering the threshold, and the choice belongs to whoever owns the consequences — not to the person who fitted the model. That hand-off is the discussion prompt two slides from now.
Case study · our running example, deciding

Real object, or false alarm?

Every frame the car processes, this question gets asked a few dozen times — and a wrong answer costs something different in each direction.

1
FeaturesDetector confidence, bounding-box height
2
LabelReal object (1) vs. false alarm (0)
3
BoundaryPlot it — then move the threshold and watch it slide
The asymmetry is the whole engineering problem. A false alarm means the car brakes for a plastic bag: annoying, occasionally dangerous for the driver behind. A miss means the car doesn't brake for a person. Those two errors are not worth the same, so 0.5 is a default, not a decision — and no amount of extra training data will choose it for you.
This is where classification stops being a maths lesson. The threshold is a value judgement wearing a number's clothes; students who leave with only that have got their money's worth. Lab 2 makes them move it and read what breaks — recall climbs from 0.62 to 0.88 as the threshold drops from 0.7 to 0.3, and precision pays for it.

Fit a classifier in Colab, five lines

The same shape as last week's regression demo — three of the five lines are literally identical. Then the two that aren't: probabilities, and the confusion matrix.

You ▸ X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42, stratify=y)
You ▸ clf = LogisticRegression(); clf.fit(X_train, y_train)
You ▸ probs = clf.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1] # ← the number the label came from
You ▸ print(confusion_matrix(y_test, clf.predict(X_test)))
You ▸ print(classification_report(y_test, clf.predict(X_test))) # precision, recall, per class

colab.research.google.com
Two things to point at explicitly. stratify=y keeps the class ratio the same in both splits — on imbalanced data, without it, a test set can end up with almost none of the rare class and the score becomes noise. And predict_proba is the line most students skip; predict() is just predict_proba with a hard-coded 0.5 in it.

Reference · key terms

Today's vocabulary

Classification — predicting which category a row belongs to. Binary = exactly two.

Sigmoid σ(z) — squashes any real number into (0, 1) so it can be read as a probability.

Decision boundary — the set of points where the model is exactly 50/50; a line in 2D, a plane in higher dimensions.

Threshold — the probability cut-off that turns 0.63 into a decision. Defaults to 0.5; should be chosen, not inherited.

Cross-entropy (log loss) — classification's loss function: heavy punishment for confident wrong answers.

Precision / recall — of my alarms, how many were real? / of the real ones, how many did I catch?

Precision and recall get a working definition today and a full hour in Class 14 — students only need to be able to say which question each one answers. Threshold is the word to over-teach; it's the one that carries the ethical weight.

Three things to keep

Backup · beyond yes/no

What if there are more than two classes?

One-vs-rest

Train one binary classifier per class — "pedestrian vs. everything", "car vs. everything", "cyclist vs. everything" — and take whichever is most confident. Simple, and it's what sklearn often does under the hood.

Softmax

One model, one score per class, all squashed together so they sum to 1.0 — the sigmoid's big sibling. This is exactly the layer at the end of nearly every modern neural network, including the ones that classify images and pick the next word.

The code barely changes — pass y with three labels instead of two and LogisticRegression handles it. What does change: your confusion matrix grows from 2×2 to 3×3, and "accuracy" gets even less informative.
Softmax is worth naming even though it isn't taught here — it's the direct bridge from tonight to Class 13's neural network and to how an LLM picks its next token. That continuity is genuinely motivating for the students who are here because of ChatGPT.
Backup · the four boxes, named

Precision and recall, properly

The confusion matrix

         said NO  said YES
truly NO    TN        FP
truly YES   FN        TP

FP = false alarm. FN = a miss. They are not the same mistake and should never share a number.

The two questions

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
"When I raise the alarm, how often am I right?"

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
"Of everything I should have caught, how much did I catch?"

Lowering the threshold buys recall and spends precision. There is no setting that maximises both.

Our lab, live: threshold 0.3 → recall 0.88, precision 0.88, one real object missed. Threshold 0.9 → precision 1.00 (every alarm correct) but recall stuck at 0.62 — three real objects missed. Same model, same data, different values.
The mnemonic that sticks: precision is about the alarms you raised, recall is about the things that were out there. Class 14 adds F1 and the precision-recall curve; today the trade-off itself is enough.
